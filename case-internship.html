Internship: unconscious bias in conversational AI

I ran a user study to find where a digital assistant falls short for speakers of African American Vernacular English, and turned what we learned into guidelines for a more inclusive model.

Role
UX design intern, researcher
When
May to Aug 2022
Method
Moderated user study, 12 participants

Timeline

One summer, May to August 2022, from first question to final presentation.

The problem

A digital assistant is only as inclusive as the data it learns from. To understand every English speaker, it has to be trained on a wide range of utterances from a diverse group of users. So the question I worked on: how do we find biases in a conversational AI and design an inclusive model?

World1.5BEnglish speakers, 160 major dialects

The study

A moderated user study, built to see how people really talk to the assistant.

01

Scope

I started with the dialects used across the US, then focused on one for this project: African American Vernacular English (AAVE).

02

Research statement

If the assistant accurately recognizes and responds to written utterances from the group, the model is inclusive of that group.

03

Sessions

One-hour moderated sessions with 12 AAVE participants, each interacting with Oracle Digital Assistant.

04

Scenarios

Expense tasks: uploading a receipt, creating an expense without one, modifying a previous expense, and creating reports.

What we found

Participants rated their experience with the digital assistant 7 out of 10. What shaped it most:

  • People code-switch. Most change how they speak depending on the environment.
  • Tone sets the tone. Responses were largely influenced by the style and tone of the assistant.
  • Money makes people careful. They speak carefully when working with money, prefer to be concise, and communicate differently when speaking than when writing.
“I choose to speak in a different way when I am in a work setting vs when I am home or talking to my friends.”
“Once the DA responded create after I clicked create, I knew the conversation would not be as organic as I would have if the greeting had been more conversational.”

Usability feedback from the same sessions:

  • Some instructions were too wordy.
  • Users want to know how long approval takes.
  • Voice input listens for too short a time.
  • Payment method should offer more options.
  • The assistant could not follow the context when the user moved off topic.

Challenges. More than half of the invited participants did not show up, some chose to speak a certain way with the assistant, and the Expense skill has little room for conversation.

Recommendations

Five guideline highlights for building a more inclusive assistant:

  • Use more natural language that people can relate to when writing responses.
  • Vary responses to the same question, for a natural feel to the conversation flow.
  • Test models with a diverse range of data, with both corporate and informal words.
  • Diversify data collection, data handling and processing.
  • Collect and compare utterances from voice and text to see efficiency.

Next steps. Re-train the model with the utterances collected, document the usability feedback so bugs can be filed, and write up the guidelines as best practices. Going forward: a bigger study with different skills and more diverse user groups, and a metric to assess the model for inclusivity.
